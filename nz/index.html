The causal chain (my Pettis balance-sheet lens)

Net migration→Population→GDP ↑ (not per head)→Imports ↑ (≈25% of spending)→Current-account deficit
Population→Housing demand→House prices→Mortgage debt→Offshore bank funding = how the deficit is financed
Population growth→Capital dilution→Productivity per head ↓, real wages ↓
OCR→House prices, credit, NZD→Import prices → CPI→Real wages

The deficit has to be absorbed somewhere. Either household debt keeps rising faster than income (the 2015–21 path), or, once migration and mortgage growth slow, demand falls short and unemployment rises (the 2023–26 path). The adjustment panel shows which one your scenario implies.

Market-oriented counterpoint: a deficit is also a capital-account surplus. Foreign savers financing NZ investment is not a problem in itself. The housing link is strong mainly because supply is rationed by zoning, consenting and infrastructure funding. If supply responded freely, migration would bring more building rather than more leverage.

This is a scenario model, not a forecast. It uses simple, transparent equations with elasticities that are either estimated from 2015–26 annual data (about 10 points, so treat them as rough), taken from published RBNZ research, or plainly labelled as assumptions. You can change all of them under Model assumptions. This is not financial advice.

Your scenario (model)

Debt/GDP is RBNZ C5 housing lending divided by Stats NZ nominal GDP (year ended June). Debt means mortgage debt grew faster than GDP (+0.5pp or more of GDP) while unemployment was flat or falling. Unemployment means debt/GDP was flat or falling while unemployment rose (+0.2pp or more). Both means both rose, and easing means neither did. This classification is a simple heuristic of mine.

Household squeeze

Real wage growth = wage growth − CPI inflation

What the scenario implies

The import effect is about 25% of household spending going on imports, multiplied by imported-price inflation above 2%. Import prices move with the NZD through the pass-through elasticity. Nominal wages follow 2% expected inflation, plus productivity per head, minus slack (unemployment above 4.5%).
