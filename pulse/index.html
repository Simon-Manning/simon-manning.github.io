Macroeconomics

Week in a nutshell. US tech had its sharpest one-day drop since 2020: on 5 November the Nasdaq 100 fell about 5–6% and the S&P 500 erased about $3 trillion, as investors priced tighter AI regulation after the Democratic midterm sweep. The real economy looked firmer. October payrolls rose 165,000 and unemployment fell to 4.1%; US productivity rose at a 3.5% annualised pace in the September quarter; and core capital-goods shipments jumped 11.6% year on year to a record $86 billion. Inflation is still eating real wages — prices are up 31.3% since April 2020, versus 25.9% for hourly earnings — and Cleveland Fed president Beth Hammack again called for gradual rate hikes. December hike odds fell after the crash, then the jobs report pushed them back up.

Pettis lens

Week in a nutshell. China’s planning chief, Zheng Shanjie, this week described the problem as “strong supply and weak demand” — official language for Pettis’s household-underconsumption diagnosis. The People’s Bank of China will raise the foreign-exchange reserve requirement by two percentage points, to 6%, from 15 November, a move reported to pull about $24 billion of dollars out of the market and slow the yuan’s rise. In Pettis’s terms that is a choice to protect producers: an appreciating currency would cheapen imports (a hidden subsidy to households) and tax exporters. China’s 10-year government bond yield hit a record low of 1.565%, consistent with excess savings seeking safety rather than households absorbing what the country produces. Pascal Lamy, a long-time free-trader, told Caixin that China’s industrial subsidies, weak domestic consumption and supply-chain price power have distorted open trade — and Pettis called it a good sign that even free-trade advocates now distinguish comparative from competitive advantage. On his Substack he argued that hopes of a technological breakthrough are more an excuse to postpone rebalancing than a reason it can wait, and he repeated that the household share of GDP can rise only if the business or government share falls. In the United States, the absorber of last resort, real household wages are still being squeezed while capital-goods shipments and labour productivity jumped — so some of this year’s investment may be creating real value, not only recycling surplus-country savings into asset prices. The midterm-week tech sell-off tightened financial conditions through markets rather than through a rise in US unemployment (which fell to 4.1%). Elsewhere, energy-driven inflation prompted the Reserve Bank of Australia to raise its cash rate to 4.85% and the Bank of England to 4%, another round of adjustment that lands on mortgagors in deficit countries.
A market-oriented counterpoint. Where Pettis leans toward managed trade or US capital-account intervention, a more market-oriented reading would put the distortion on China’s side of the ledger: the PBOC stopping the yuan from rising is itself the policy that keeps income away from Chinese households. Letting the currency appreciate, freeing deposit rates and enforcing harder budget constraints on state firms would raise the household share without the US adding tariffs or capital controls, which create their own rents and retaliation. The identity S − I = CA still holds; the disagreement is whether the fix is more state management in deficit countries or fewer repression-style distortions in surplus ones. This week’s US productivity gain also cautions against treating all of the capex boom as non-productive absorption — some of it may be paying for itself.

New Zealand

Week in a nutshell. The standout official release was the labour market. Goods trade, the dollar, export prices and mortgage rates also moved, or were updated, around households this week.

The latest goods-trade figures are still those for September, published on 19 October. Stats NZ recorded a monthly deficit of $2.0 billion: exports rose 12% on a year earlier, to $6.5 billion, while imports rose 18%, to $8.4 billion. Petroleum and petroleum products were $1.2 billion of those imports, up $608 million or 96% on September 2025, “predominately driven by higher prices.” For the year to September the goods deficit was about $6.2 billion. The broader balance-of-payments goods-and-services balance, year-ended June 2026, was −$2.95 billion, and the current-account deficit was 3.2% of GDP, or −$14.58 billion (Stats NZ, Overseas merchandise trade: September 2026, 19 Oct 2026; Balance of payments, June 2026 quarter, 16 Sep 2026).

The New Zealand dollar is historically weak. On 6 November the Reserve Bank’s 17-currency trade-weighted index was 63.66 and one kiwi bought US$0.55960, after an October monthly-average TWI of 64.05 and US$0.5632 (RBNZ B1, 6 Nov 2026). A weaker kiwi supports exporters, but it raises the cost of the roughly 24% of household consumption that is imported goods.

That import-price pressure already shows up in the terms of trade. In the June 2026 quarter the goods terms of trade fell 9.0% on the quarter and 9.4% on the year, because import prices jumped — especially petroleum — not because export prices collapsed (Stats NZ, International trade: June 2026 quarter, 3 Sep 2026). Some offset arrived this week on the export side: ANZ’s world commodity-price index rose 0.6% in October (up 2.0% on the year), and the NZ-dollar index rose 2.5% because the kiwi weakened after the Reserve Bank’s late-October decision to leave the OCR unchanged. Dairy prices rose 1.6% on the month; skim milk powder was up 5.9% on the month and 51.6% on the year, while butter fell 3.3% and 29.3% respectively (ANZ Commodity Price Index, 4 Nov 2026).

Productivity per person is still barely moving. Real GDP per capita was $13,410 in the June 2026 quarter (2009/10 prices), up just 0.08% on the quarter and still below the September 2022 peak of $13,718. There was no new GDP or productivity release this week (Stats NZ, GDP: June 2026 quarter, 17 Sep 2026).

Household mortgage costs have stopped falling in any meaningful way. The OCR remains 2.75% after the late-October hold; the next review is on 9 December. Carded standard rates at the end of October, published on 6 November, were 6.43% floating (unchanged from September) and 5.54% for a one-year fix (5.55% in September) (RBNZ B2; RBNZ B20, 6 Nov 2026). Housing lending was $404.76 billion in September, up 5.4% on the year (RBNZ C5, 30 Oct 2026). House prices remain roughly flat: the CoreLogic index via the Reserve Bank was 3,365 in the June 2026 quarter, down 0.3% on the year (RBNZ M10, 9 Oct 2026).

NZ labour & migration tracker

Thesis check: supports. This week’s standout release is the 4 November labour-market report. Unemployment rose to 5.7% — the highest since September 2015 — with 174,000 people out of work, while employment fell by about 5,000 even as the working-age population rose by about 13,000. Real average hourly earnings are still falling (2.89% versus CPI of 3.71%). At the same time, net migration is rising again (an annual gain of 20,500 in the year to August, up from 9,200 a year earlier) and work-visa approvals were 20,199 in September. That combination — more unemployed New Zealanders, weak real wages, and still-easy migrant hiring — supports the labour-market half of the thesis. The housing-and-trade half is less newly confirmed this week. Mortgage debt is still growing (5.4% year on year, to $405 billion in September), which fits the idea that housing credit absorbs the external deficit, but house prices are roughly flat rather than inflating, and September’s $2.0 billion goods-trade deficit was driven mainly by a near-doubling of the fuel-import bill, not by a fresh migration spike. The kiwi is already weak, so households are paying more for the imported share of spending even without a further migration-driven move in the currency.
