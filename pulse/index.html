Macroeconomics

Week in a nutshell. Inflation is back at the centre. US consumer prices rose 0.4% in October (core 0.3%), both above forecasts, and producer prices were up 6.5% on the year, the highest since November 2022. Markets moved to near-certain odds of a December Fed hike, with a small chance of 50 basis points, and Goldman added a third hike to its forecast. The US 10-year Treasury yield went back above 5% and ended the week at about 5.15%. Nvidia’s chief executive Jensen Huang resigned amid a scandal, which knocked AI stocks before dip-buyers stepped in. Trade policy stayed noisy: President Trump threatened to double tariffs on Mexico, and Charlie Bilello pointed out that $128 billion of tariffs were refunded to companies without any refund to consumers. China’s October exports rose 35.2% on the year and its imports 41.6%.

Pettis lens

Week in a nutshell. China’s October numbers read like a page from Pettis’s notes. Consumer prices rose just 0.7% on the year and were flat on the month — “given weak consumption… this doesn’t come as a surprise,” he wrote — while producer prices rose 5.7%, a four-year high, so the gap keeps widening: firms are passing on higher input costs but households aren’t bidding up consumer goods. New yuan loans fell by about ¥100 billion in October, the first October contraction on record (interest.co.nz, 16 Nov). When the People’s Bank argued that its loan rates are already low by world standards, Pettis pushed back that the binding problem is weak consumer loan demand, not the price of credit. He flagged two signs of supply-side bias: labour-intensive exports are slowing even though China has among the lowest unit labour costs in the world, and plans to extend the high-speed rail network by 40% into more economically marginal regions look driven more by investment targets than economic need. On pensions, he said the household share of GDP must keep rising to fund an ageing population, which is the rebalancing question again. His latest Substack essay (7 Nov) argues that hoping for a technological breakthrough is a way to postpone that rebalancing. On the deficit side, the United States is absorbing China’s surplus with rising rates and a record $5.19 trillion of consumer credit, and Pettis called President Trump’s threat to double tariffs on Mexico “nonsense” that makes US trade policy’s impact “either zero or negative.” He also said Russia’s retreat from using the yuan fits his long-standing argument about surplus countries; as he put it in a reply, Russia cannot balance its trade surplus with ruble assets.
A market-oriented counterpoint. Much of what Pettis diagnosed this week is state-made: credit steered into marginal rail lines, local investment targets, and a central bank leaning against the yuan. A market-oriented fix is to remove those distortions — harder budget constraints for state firms and local governments, market-set deposit rates, and a freer currency — so that more income flows to households without a new round of administered transfers. Guo Shuqing’s idea of moving state-owned capital into the pension system fits both camps, because it shrinks state ownership while raising household income. On tariffs there was little to dispute: free-traders and Pettis alike see the Mexico threat as costly to the United States.

New Zealand

Week in a nutshell. The kiwi slid to its lowest against the US dollar since March 2009 as US yields jumped, migration figures showed net gains rising again, and card spending fell for a third month as fuel bills crowded out other purchases. Banks trimmed some fixed mortgage rates, even as their economists warn rates are heading up.

No new goods-trade figures this week; October’s are due shortly. The latest are still for September, published on 19 October: a monthly deficit of $2.0 billion, with exports up 12% on a year earlier, to $6.5 billion, and imports up 18%, to $8.4 billion. Petroleum imports were $1.2 billion, up 96%, “predominately driven by higher prices.” The goods deficit for the year to September was about $6.2 billion. The year-ended goods-and-services balance to June 2026 was −$2.95 billion, and the current-account deficit was 3.2% of GDP, or −$14.58 billion (Stats NZ, Overseas merchandise trade: September 2026, 19 Oct 2026; Balance of payments, June 2026 quarter, 16 Sep 2026).

The New Zealand dollar fell further. On 16 November one kiwi bought just under US$0.553, its lowest since March 2009, and the five-currency TWI was about 59.4, a 17-year low. The US dollar is at a 19-month high on rising US interest rates, with the US 10-year yield at 5.15%, up 30 basis points in the week (interest.co.nz breakfast briefing, 16 Nov 2026). The Reserve Bank’s October monthly averages were a 17-currency TWI of 64.05 and US$0.5632 (RBNZ B1, 6 Nov 2026). For households, a weaker kiwi raises the cost of the roughly 24% of consumption that is imported goods, and fuel is the clearest example: October card spending on fuel rose 4.8% while core retail spending fell 1.2% ($73 million), the third monthly fall in a row, with durables down 2.1% and hospitality down 1.6% (Stats NZ, Electronic card transactions: October 2026, 12 Nov 2026). ASB’s Yen Nguyen said high fuel prices are “taxing consumption” (interest.co.nz, 12 Nov 2026). Westpac noted that its own card data was stronger than the official series.

The terms of trade are the offset. Export prices for dairy are firm: at the 3 November Global Dairy Trade auction the index rose 3% to an average US$4,166 a tonne, with whole milk powder up 5.9% and skim milk powder up 3.1%, while butter fell 4.7% (RNZ, 4 Nov 2026); the next auction is on 18 November. ANZ’s commodity index rose 0.6% in October, and 2.5% in NZ-dollar terms (ANZ Commodity Price Index, 4 Nov 2026). But the latest official goods terms of trade, for the June 2026 quarter, fell 9.0% on the quarter and 9.4% on the year because import prices, especially petroleum, jumped (Stats NZ, International trade: June 2026 quarter, 3 Sep 2026). A record-low kiwi helps exporters’ NZ-dollar returns but makes the fuel bill heavier.

Productivity per person has not been updated: real GDP per capita was $13,410 in the June 2026 quarter (2009/10 prices), up 0.08% on the quarter and still below the September 2022 peak of $13,718 (Stats NZ, GDP: June 2026 quarter, 17 Sep 2026). September-quarter GDP is due in December.

Mortgages: the OCR is 2.75% after October’s 4–3 hold, and the next review is on 9 December. Banks are competing at the short end: from 11 November Westpac cut its one-year special by 20 basis points to 4.99% and trimmed its three-, four- and five-year rates, but raised its two-year special to 5.39%, and said its economists “still think interest rates are heading upwards over the next year” (1News, 10 Nov 2026). The NZ 10-year government bond yield was 4.85%, up 6 basis points on the week (interest.co.nz, 16 Nov 2026). Carded standard rates at the end of October were 6.43% floating and 5.54% for a one-year fix (RBNZ B20, 6 Nov 2026). Housing lending was $404.76 billion in September, up 5.4% on the year (RBNZ C5, 30 Oct 2026), and house prices are roughly flat, down 0.3% on the year in the June quarter (RBNZ M10, 9 Oct 2026). The Reserve Bank’s Financial Stability Report is due this week.

NZ labour & migration tracker

Thesis check: doesn’t change it (supports the labour half, challenges the currency link). The new figure this week is migration. Net migration rose to 22,600 in the year to September, up from 9,900 a year earlier, made up of a net gain of 60,400 non-New Zealand citizens and a net loss of 37,800 New Zealand citizens. That is happening while unemployment is at an 11-year high of 5.7% (174,000 people), real hourly earnings are still falling (2.89% against CPI of 3.71%), work-visa approvals were 20,199 in September, and card spending has fallen for three months. Rising inflows of non-citizen workers into a slack labour market, alongside falling real wages, support the labour-market half of the thesis. The currency and housing half is challenged this week: the kiwi fell to a 17-year low even as migration rose, because US interest rates and the US dollar are driving it, and house prices are flat. Mortgage lending is still growing about 5% a year, which fits the idea that housing credit funds the external deficit, but this week’s data gives no sign that migration is propping up the NZD or house prices. Overall the week doesn’t change the thesis: it strengthens the labour case and weakens the near-term currency link.
